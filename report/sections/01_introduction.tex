\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

The visual field is mapped onto the surface of early visual cortex in a
topographic manner known as a retinotopic map. Each cortical location is assigned
two visual-field coordinates, eccentricity (distance from the fovea, the centre
of gaze) and polar angle (direction), and neighbouring visual-field positions map to neighbouring
cortical positions~\cite{wandell2007,sereno1995borders}. The mapping is
non-uniform: through cortical magnification the cortex devotes disproportionately
more surface to the fovea than to the periphery~\cite{cowey1974,schwartz1980}, so
the map is continuous but geometrically warped. Because this functional
organisation is closely tied to cortical anatomy, it can in principle be predicted
from structure alone.

\subsection{Motivation}
\label{sec:motivation}

Retinotopic maps are conventionally obtained through functional MRI, in which
controlled visual stimuli are presented while cortical responses are recorded and
fitted with a population receptive field (pRF) model~\cite{dumoulin2008}. Such
mapping is accurate but expensive in scan time, impractical for some populations,
and must be repeated for every individual. This has motivated computational models
that infer the retinotopic map instead of measuring it in full for each
subject~\cite{ribeiro2021}, reducing the amount of functional data required.

Our contribution concerns the geometry such models operate on rather than the
prediction target itself. These models represent the cortical surface as a mesh of
vertices (discrete surface points) and aggregate information over neighbouring
vertices using distances measured with a Euclidean (flat) metric. Yet cortical
magnification warps the retinotopic map, so that equal steps across the visual
field do not correspond to equal steps across the cortex~\cite{cowey1974,schwartz1980},
a flat metric therefore matches this warped geometry only approximately. This
suggests measuring inter-vertex distances with a curved metric that follows the
same non-uniformity, and may thereby align better with the underlying anatomy. We
ask whether a curved rather than a Euclidean metric recovers the warped map more
accurately, and we study this in a controlled synthetic setting where the
ground-truth map is known exactly (Section~\ref{sec:data}).

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

Retinotopic organisation has traditionally been estimated by warping analytic
templates to an individual's cortical anatomy, which capture coarse structure but
assume a fixed structure--function relationship and cannot express finer
individual variation. \citet{ribeiro2021} removed this assumption using geometric
deep learning, applying spline-based (B-spline) convolutions directly to the
cortical surface mesh to predict eccentricity and polar angle at every vertex.
Within their model, the surface convolution measures the relative positions of
neighbouring vertices with a Euclidean metric on their 3D coordinates. We take
this model as our baseline and change only this metric, replacing it with a
constant-curvature (hyperbolic or spherical) metric controlled by a single
parameter $\kappa$. Setting $\kappa = 0$ recovers the Euclidean (flat) metric,
matching the choice of~\citet{ribeiro2021}, so that within our experiments any
difference in performance is attributable to the curvature alone, with the
architecture and training objective otherwise unchanged. Our setup also differs in
the network input: rather than the anatomical features (cortical curvature and
myelin) used by~\citet{ribeiro2021}, we drive the network with simulated pRF
responses defined on synthetic retinotopic maps, which gives full control over the
known ground-truth geometry (Section~\ref{sec:data}).
